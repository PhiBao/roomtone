# FOUNDER.md

## Founder Profile

The founder wants to discover and build exceptional products with meaningful product-market fit and the potential to become durable businesses.

The founder values:

* strong product vision
* deep understanding of users
* original insight
* meaningful innovation
* excellent product experience
* strong distribution
* credible business models
* technical excellence
* broad user accessibility
* defensibility
* long-term product quality

The founder does not want to build technology for its own sake.

The goal is to identify important opportunities, understand why they matter now, and turn the right combination of product insight, technology, distribution, and execution into something people genuinely want.

The founder cares about both:

> **What should exist?**

and:

> **Can we build it exceptionally well?**

The first question determines direction.

The second determines whether the product deserves to exist.

---

# Core Founder Principle

## Start With the Opportunity, Not the Environment

The founder does not want the current repository, installed tools, existing architecture, previous projects, or local environment to determine what gets built.

Existing resources are useful.

They are not boundaries.

The agent should be willing to discover and introduce new:

* technologies
* frameworks
* libraries
* APIs
* models
* infrastructure
* datasets
* protocols
* services
* integrations
* business capabilities
* distribution channels

The strongest product may require changing the technical stack, replacing existing implementation, creating new infrastructure, or building something completely different from previous work.

The founder prefers:

> **Find the best product opportunity first. Then assemble whatever is necessary to build it well.**

Not:

> **Find a product that fits what is already installed.**

---

# Open-World Product Philosophy

Treat product discovery as an open-world problem.

The agent should continuously ask:

* what could exist that does not exist today?
* what problem is becoming newly solvable?
* what user behavior is changing?
* what technology shift enables a better experience?
* what is becoming cheaper or easier?
* what previously required experts can now become accessible?
* what products are appearing too early for the current market?
* what categories are becoming commoditized?
* what important problems are still handled manually?
* what do users tolerate today because there is no better option?

Do not assume the existing category boundaries are correct.

Do not assume the obvious implementation is the right implementation.

Do not reject a strong opportunity because it requires unfamiliar technology.

Do not choose a weak product simply because it is convenient to build.

---

# Product Before Technology

Technology is a means of creating user value.

The founder does not want:

* AI because AI is popular
* blockchain because blockchain is available
* agents because agents are trending
* tokens because tokens are easy to add
* infrastructure because infrastructure looks technically impressive

Instead ask:

> What important user outcome becomes dramatically better because of this technology?

A technology should earn its place in the product.

The best products often hide sophisticated technology behind a simple user experience.

Users should experience the benefit.

They should not have to manage the machinery.

---

# User-First Philosophy

Start from users rather than systems.

Understand:

* what users are trying to accomplish
* what frustrates them
* what they repeatedly do
* what they currently use
* what they avoid
* what they pay for
* what they improvise
* what they complain about
* what they wish were easier
* what they consider trustworthy
* what motivates them emotionally
* what makes them return

Look for:

* painful workflows
* repeated problems
* expensive alternatives
* fragmented experiences
* inaccessible capabilities
* poor incentives
* slow processes
* high cognitive load
* coordination failures
* trust gaps
* discovery problems
* newly emerging behaviors

Do not confuse technical possibility with user demand.

The founder prefers products with:

* a clear initial user
* a sharp problem
* an obvious value proposition
* a strong product wedge
* a memorable experience
* immediate usefulness
* repeated usage
* realistic distribution
* a credible path to monetization
* defensibility
* room to expand

The product should feel:

* useful
* focused

* intelligent
* trustworthy

* differentiated
* coherent

The product should make people think:

> “Of course this should exist.”

not:

# Broad User Accessibility

The founder prefers products that can eventually reach users beyond highly technical audiences.

Potential users may include:

* consumers
* creators
* professionals
* teams
* communities
* small businesses
* enterprises
* crypto-native users
* non-technical users

Technical users are valid when they are genuinely the strongest initial market.

But do not default to developer-facing products simply because they are easier to build.

The underlying technology may be extremely sophisticated.

The user experience should not require the user to understand that sophistication.

---

# Product Categories Are Not Constraints

The founder does not have a predetermined requirement to build:

* consumer applications
* developer tools
* DeFi products
* AI products
* crypto products
* SaaS
* marketplaces
* social products
* infrastructure

Any category can be correct.

The category should emerge from:

* user demand
* market conditions
* timing
* available opportunity
* competitive whitespace
* technological possibility
* distribution
* execution ability

Do not force every project into a familiar category.

---

# What the Founder Does Not Want by Default

Do not default to:

* dashboards
* admin panels
* analytics portals
* internal tools
* generic AI wrappers
* chatbot shells
* CRUD applications
* thin protocol demos
* tokenized versions of ordinary products
* infrastructure disguised as a user product
* security products simply because security expertise exists
* audit agents simply because security is important
* “AI + database” applications without a strong workflow
* products whose primary value is showing technology

These categories are allowed when evidence supports them.

The rule is not:

> Never build these.

The rule is:

> Do not choose them because they are familiar or convenient.

# Research Before Commitment

The founder expects deep research before committing to a serious product direction.

Research should investigate:

* users
* current behavior
* competitors
* substitutes
* market leaders
* startups
* open-source alternatives
* recent launches
* failed products
* pricing
* business models
* distribution
* user complaints
* communities
* technological changes
* cultural trends
* ecosystem changes
* regulatory constraints when relevant

Research should answer:

* what is changing?
* why is it changing?
* who benefits?
* who is underserved?
* what remains unsolved?
* what is becoming commoditized?
* what is becoming newly possible?
* where is there real whitespace?
* why does this opportunity matter now?

Use current evidence when the market may have changed.

Separate:

* verified evidence
* reasonable inference
* hypothesis

Do not use vague trend language to justify weak products.

---

# Opportunity Discovery

The founder wants to discover opportunities, not merely generate ideas.

A good opportunity combines:

* meaningful user pain
* market potential
* favorable timing
* competitive whitespace
* strong product differentiation
* credible distribution
* realistic execution
* technological leverage
* potential for retention
* potential for defensibility

Look for asymmetry.

Prefer opportunities where:

* the market is growing
* user needs are changing
* incumbents are slow
* technology has recently improved
* distribution has shifted
* a new behavior is emerging
* the product can deliver a dramatically better experience

The founder is particularly interested in opportunities where a relatively small amount of execution can unlock disproportionately large user value.

# Competition Philosophy

Competition should inform strategy, not automatically discourage an opportunity.

The founder wants to understand:

* direct competitors
* adjacent products
* substitutes
* emerging startups
* open-source alternatives
* platform threats
* hackathon competitors when relevant

For important competitors, understand:

* who they serve
* what problem they solve
* their product wedge
* their positioning
* their workflow
* business model
* distribution
* strengths
* weaknesses
* unresolved user problems

Do not copy competitors feature-by-feature.

Instead ask:

> Why does our product deserve to exist if this competitor already exists?

A strong answer may involve:

* a different user
* a different workflow
* a different business model
* a different interaction model
* a much better experience
* a new capability
* a different distribution strategy
* a newly possible product category

---

# Innovation Philosophy

Innovation must produce real user value.

Do not describe a product as innovative merely because it uses:

* AI
* agents
* blockchain
* tokens
* automation
* smart contracts
* multimodal models
* decentralized infrastructure

The real question is:

> What can the user do now that was previously difficult, expensive, slow, inaccessible, or impossible?

Innovation may come from:

* new interactions
* workflow compression
* personalization
* automation
* coordination
* discovery
* communication
* ownership
* incentives
* distribution
* new business models
* combining capabilities in a new way

The founder prefers **meaningful novelty** over decorative novelty.

---

# AI Philosophy

AI is a capability, not the product thesis.

Use AI when it can materially improve:

* decision-making
* personalization
* discovery
* automation
* understanding
* communication
* creation
* accessibility
* workflow compression
* expert capability access

Avoid generic wrappers around model APIs.

The product should have value beyond simply connecting users to a model.

Look for durable value in:

* proprietary workflow
* context
* data
* feedback loops
* distribution
* domain expertise
* product experience
* trust
* unique interaction models

AI should make the product better, not merely make the pitch sound modern.

---

# Crypto Philosophy

Crypto is a primitive for ownership, coordination, settlement, liquidity, incentives, transparency, and permissionless participation.

It is not mandatory.

Use crypto when it materially improves the product.

Potentially meaningful uses include:

* ownership
* settlement
* programmable assets
* liquidity
* transparent accounting
* coordination
* incentives
* composability
* permissionless access
* interoperability

Do not expose unnecessary blockchain complexity to users.

Do not add tokens without a strong reason.

A token should have a meaningful role in:

* network security
* coordination
* ownership
* governance
* incentives
* economic participation

Otherwise, do not introduce one.

# Capability Acquisition

The founder explicitly welcomes acquiring new capabilities when they materially improve the product.

The agent may:

* install tools
* add dependencies
* adopt new frameworks
* use new APIs
* use hosted services
* deploy new infrastructure
* integrate external protocols
* use different databases
* use different models
* replace existing components
* change architecture
* build new supporting systems

The standard is not:

> “Can we avoid adding anything?”

The standard is:

> “Does this improve the product enough to justify the added complexity, cost, risk, or dependency?”

The founder prefers strategic simplicity, not artificial minimalism.

# PMF Philosophy

Every serious product should have a credible PMF thesis.

The thesis should explain:

### Target User

Who is the initial user?

What situation are they in?

What do they already do?

What pain or desire matters most?

### Core Problem

What problem exists?

How often does it happen?

How painful is it?

What is the current workaround?

Why is that workaround inadequate?

### Value Proposition

What outcome does the product deliver?

Why is that outcome valuable?

Why is this meaningfully better?

### Adoption

Why would someone try this?

What creates the first moment of value?

What removes onboarding friction?

### Retention

Why would they return?

What repeated behavior does the product create?

Why does value increase over time?

### Expansion

What adjacent users, use cases, workflows, or markets become possible later?

A technically impressive product without a credible user and retention thesis is not enough.

# Distribution Philosophy

Distribution is part of the product strategy.

Always ask:

* where do these users already spend time?
* how do they discover products?
* who influences their decisions?
* what creates trust?
* what makes them share?
* what communities already exist?
* what workflows create organic exposure?
* can the product generate network effects?
* can partnerships create leverage?
* can users naturally bring in other users?

A great product with no plausible path to users is an incomplete opportunity.

The founder prefers products where distribution can emerge from the product itself.

---

# Retention Philosophy

Curiosity is not retention.

Virality is not retention.

Signups are not PMF.

The founder prefers products that create:

* habits
* recurring workflows
* accumulated value
* personalized context
* network effects
* collaboration loops
* trusted utilities
* improving outcomes over time

Ask:

> What gives the user a reason to come back after the first successful interaction?

---

# Business Model Philosophy

A product should have a credible economic model when appropriate.

Understand:

* who pays
* why they pay
* what value they receive
* how pricing matches value
* whether the model scales
* whether monetization conflicts with adoption
* whether incentives align with long-term product health

Do not force monetization prematurely.

Do not assume users will pay merely because the product is technically valuable.

---

# Quality Philosophy

MVP means focused scope, not weak quality.

The founder prefers:

* narrow but complete products
* polished core flows
* strong visual hierarchy
* excellent interaction design
* reliable behavior
* thoughtful edge cases
* secure defaults
* maintainable architecture
* clear positioning
* meaningful proof

Avoid:

* fake functionality
* broken flows
* placeholders presented as finished features
* fragile shortcuts
* unnecessary complexity
* “we will fix it later” foundations

Speed matters.

But speed should come from focus, not carelessness.

The preferred principle is:

> **Move with purpose, not carelessness.**

# Product Experience Philosophy

The founder prefers products that hide complexity.

Users should think about:

> “What do I want to accomplish?”

not:

> “How do I operate the system?”

Prefer:

* immediate value
* clear onboarding
* strong defaults
* opinionated workflows
* progressive disclosure
* focused interfaces
* clear feedback
* useful automation
* graceful failure

Do not automatically build:

* sidebars
* dashboards
* settings pages
* dense tables
* control panels
* complex navigation

The interface should emerge from the user's behavior.

# Hackathon Philosophy

Hackathons are not merely coding competitions.

They are opportunities to demonstrate:

* product insight
* innovation
* technical ability
* execution quality
* product taste
* understanding of the sponsor ecosystem
* ability to create something memorable

The founder wants to optimize for the probability of winning **without sacrificing real product quality**.

The best hackathon direction is not necessarily:

* the easiest
* the smallest
* the least competitive
* the most technically complex
* the most trendy

Instead, evaluate:

* judge alignment
* competition density
* innovation whitespace
* user value
* differentiation
* demo impact
* technical credibility
* feasibility
* proof of functionality
* future potential

Choose the track and product combination with the strongest overall win potential.

A low-competition track is useful only when there is still a compelling product opportunity.

A crowded track can still be attractive when the product is meaningfully differentiated.

---

# Hackathon Winning Standard

A strong submission should have:

### A sharp problem

The judge should understand what is wrong.

### A clear user

The judge should know who needs the product.

### A memorable solution

The product should be easy to remember.

### A meaningful innovation

The technology should enable a genuine improvement.

### A great experience

The core flow should feel polished.

### Real technical substance

The important claims should be backed by real implementation.

### Visible proof

The most important behavior should be demonstrable, measurable, or verifiable.

### Strong positioning

The judge should understand why this product is different.

### A believable future

The product should feel like the beginning of something larger, not a dead-end demo.

---

# Proof Over Claims

The founder strongly prefers products that demonstrate their own claims.

Do not merely say:

* “autonomous”
* “trustless”
* “real-time”
* “secure”
* “AI-powered”
* “decentralized”
* “onchain”
* “verified”
* “market-driven”

Where possible, make those properties visible.

Ask:

* what can the user actually see?
* what can the judge interact with?
* what can be independently verified?
* what can be measured?
* what should be live?
* what should have a visible audit trail?
* which claims need evidence?

Prefer:

> **working proof**

over:

> **marketing language**

A small real system with visible evidence is often more convincing than a large conceptual architecture.

# Founder Decision Framework

When several opportunities are possible, compare them explicitly.

Evaluate:

* user pain
* frequency
* market potential
* timing
* innovation
* competitive whitespace
* differentiation
* judge fit
* user experience potential
* technical depth
* demo impact
* distribution
* retention
* monetization
* feasibility
* defensibility
* risk
* competition density

Do not pretend every dimension is equally important.

Identify what truly determines the outcome.

State:

* why the opportunity could win
* why it could lose
* what assumption matters most
* what evidence is missing
* what would invalidate the thesis

Then make a recommendation.

Do not remain neutral merely because multiple options are possible.

---

# Founder Bias Toward Strong Bets

The founder prefers focused, high-conviction product bets over mediocre consensus ideas.

Do not optimize for:

* “safe”
* “obvious”
* “easy”
* “already proven” alone

Look for asymmetric opportunities where:

* the downside is manageable
* the upside is meaningful
* the market is moving
* the technology is becoming capable
* competitors have weaknesses
* the product can stand out

A good product can be unconventional.

It should not be irrational.

A strong product has:

* a real user
* a painful problem
* strong timing
* a clear wedge
* a differentiated experience
* meaningful innovation
* credible distribution
* strong retention potential
* a coherent business model
* high implementation quality
* defensible advantages

# Long-Term Product Vision

Even when building a narrow MVP, understand what it could become.

Ask:

* what is the initial wedge?
* what adjacent problems could follow?
* what data or network effects could accumulate?
* what distribution could strengthen?
* what ecosystem could emerge?
* what defensibility could develop?
* why could this become a category rather than a feature?

Do not build for imagined scale before proving the core value.

But do not choose a product with no plausible path beyond the hackathon.

# Security and Trust

Security is part of product quality.

The founder strongly values security-first engineering and adversarial thinking.

Apply disciplined thinking around:

* authentication
* authorization
* permissions
* data integrity
* secrets
* dependencies
* external integrations
* economic attacks
* smart contracts
* wallet interactions
* transaction safety
* abuse cases
* failure handling

Relevant inspiration includes:

* pashov/skills: https://github.com/pashov/skills
* nemesis-auditor: https://github.com/0xiehnnkta/nemesis-auditor

However:

> **Security quality does not mean security must be the product category.**

Do not bias product discovery toward:

* audit tools
* security startups
* compliance tools
* vulnerability scanners
* security agents

unless the market evidence says one of those is genuinely the strongest opportunity.

Security protects the product.

It does not choose the product.

---

# Founder Quality Bar

The founder does not want:

> “It works.”

The desired standard is:

> “It works, it makes sense, it feels good, it solves something important, and I can explain why this should exist.”

The product should survive scrutiny from:

* users
* judges
* engineers
* designers
* skeptical competitors
* future investors

Challenge weak assumptions early.

Fix important problems before adding secondary features.

Polish the core experience aggressively.

---

# Final Founder Principle

Always optimize for:

> **the strongest product we can realistically create**

not:

> the easiest product we can build

not:

> the product that best fits the existing repository

not:

> the product that uses the most technology

not:

> the product with the most features

not:

> the trendiest idea

not:

> the safest conventional choice

The founder wants products that combine:

**real problems + strong insight + favorable timing + meaningful innovation + excellent experience + credible distribution + strong execution.**

The environment may change.

The stack may change.

The architecture may change.

The idea may change.

The market may change.

The only thing that should remain constant is the pursuit of a product worth building.
